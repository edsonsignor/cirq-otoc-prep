5. What is your experience with Cirq?
Answer cue:
Be honest. Your Cirq experience is recent, but you have implemented toy echo circuits: random circuit U, mid-circuit measurement damage, U†, and probe recovery. Your stronger background is numerical quantum dynamics, so you can ramp quickly.

11. What is a FOTOC?
Answer cue:
A fidelity out-of-time-order correlator. It is an echo-type diagnostic: evolve forward, apply a perturbation, evolve backward, and measure sensitivity to that perturbation.

12. How is a FOTOC related to the Google protocol?
Answer cue:
Same skeleton: U, perturbation or measurement damage, U†, final recovery/sensitivity measurement. Different observable and platform, but similar diagnostic logic.

13. What is the difference between a FOTOC and an OTOC?
Answer cue:
An OTOC is usually written as an operator correlator such as <W†(t) V† W(t) V>. A FOTOC is a fidelity/echo-based diagnostic, often state-dependent. They are not identical, but both probe perturbation growth, operator spreading, and scrambling-like behavior.

============================================================
3. Core project questions
============================================================

17. Explain the project protocol.
Answer cue:
Prepare a probe qubit in |1>, apply a random circuit U, introduce a local mid-circuit measurement/damage operation, apply U†, and measure the probe recovery probability. The goal is to distinguish classical-like memory loss around 1/2 from quantum scrambling/random-unitary behavior around 3/4.

18. Why apply U†?
Answer cue:
It creates an echo. If there is no damage and no noise, U†U = I, so the initial information is recovered. Loss of recovery indicates damage, scrambling, or hardware imperfections.

19. What is the difference between a no-damage echo and a damaged echo?
Answer cue:
No-damage echo tests reversal fidelity. Damaged echo tests how much a local perturbation or measurement affects globally scrambled information.

20. Why is the mid-circuit measurement similar to a Pauli butterfly?
Answer cue:
If a Z-basis measurement outcome is discarded, the channel is:
M_Z(rho) = P0 rho P0 + P1 rho P1 = 1/2 (rho + Z rho Z).
So it is equivalent to averaging over no Pauli insertion and a Z insertion. It is OTOC-like, but still a non-unitary measurement channel.

21. What is a Kraus operator?
Answer cue:
Kraus operators describe a general quantum channel:
E(rho) = sum_k E_k rho E_k†,
with completeness sum_k E_k† E_k = I.

22. What is a POVM?
Answer cue:
A POVM describes measurement probabilities. The POVM element is M_k = E_k† E_k, and p_k = Tr(M_k rho).

23. What is the difference between selective and nonselective measurement?
Answer cue:
Selective measurement conditions on a particular outcome. Nonselective measurement discards the outcome and averages over all outcomes, giving a quantum channel.

24. What is a random circuit?
Answer cue:
A layered circuit with randomly chosen local gates and entangling gates. It is a controllable model of chaotic many-body dynamics.

25. Is a random circuit Haar-random?
Answer cue:
Not at finite depth. A finite-depth random circuit can approximate Haar-like behavior for selected observables or low-order moments, but it is not literally Haar-random.

26. What is a unitary design?
Answer cue:
An ensemble of unitaries that reproduces Haar averages up to a given moment/order. Random circuits can approximate low-order designs after sufficient depth.

27. Why 1/2 classically and 3/4 quantum mechanically?
Answer cue:
Classically, strong damage/scrambling erases the probe memory, so the final measurement is a random bit: 1/2. Quantum mechanically, in the random-unitary/RMT regime, information is hidden nonlocally and the project observable has a universal partial recovery value around 3/4.

28. What is an OTOC?
Answer cue:
An OTOC measures whether a time-evolved local operator has spread enough to fail to commute with another local operator. A standard form is <W†(t) V† W(t) V>.

29. What is butterfly velocity?
Answer cue:
The speed at which the operator-spreading front moves through the system.

30. What is the Lieb-Robinson bound?
Answer cue:
A locality bound showing that information/operator spreading in local quantum systems has an effective finite speed. It gives the light-cone intuition.

31. What is light-cone pruning?
Answer cue:
Removing gates outside the causal cone of the measured observable because those gates cannot affect the observable at that depth. It is observable-aware simplification, not generic compilation.

============================================================
4. Hardware / noise / error mitigation questions
============================================================

32. What is Pauli twirling?
Answer cue:
It randomizes the error frame so coherent or structured errors become effectively stochastic Pauli-like errors after averaging. It does not remove the error; it changes the noise model.

33. Why is coherent error dangerous?
Answer cue:
Coherent over-rotations can accumulate systematically across many gates, biasing the echo signal.

34. Why is stochastic noise easier to handle?
Answer cue:
It is easier to average, model, and use in mitigation strategies such as zero-noise extrapolation.

35. What is circuit folding?
Answer cue:
Replace U by logically equivalent longer circuits, such as U U† U, to amplify noise while preserving the ideal operation.

36. What is zero-noise extrapolation?
Answer cue:
Measure the same observable at several effective noise levels and extrapolate back to the zero-noise limit.

37. What are the caveats of ZNE?
Answer cue:
It assumes noise can be scaled in a controlled way and that the extrapolation model is valid over the sampled range. It does not by itself prove scrambling.

38. Suppose the recovery is 0.62, not 0.75. What do you check?
Answer cue:
First check no-damage echo/reversal fidelity. Then readout and mid-circuit measurement fidelity. Then two-qubit gate errors/XEB on active qubit pairs. Then compare with noisy simulation and finite-depth/finite-size ideal simulations. Only after controls interpret it as physics.

39. Suppose the no-damage echo is already bad. What does that mean?
Answer cue:
The issue is not scrambling yet. The forward-backward circuit is not being reversed well enough. Diagnose gate fidelity, calibration drift, decoherence, leakage, compilation/inversion errors.

40. Suppose the signal goes below 1/2. What could that suggest?
Answer cue:
Coherent bias, readout asymmetry, postselection/conditioning effects, calibration problems, or a wrong observable definition. Do not immediately interpret it as physical scrambling.
